Living regions

● Rest slows movement and creates a glow, but changes each species differently. ● Gather attracts neighbours and changes each species differently. ● Exchange causes vibration and regenerates shared resources; its bodily effects differ by species.

Time spent in each region changes bodies and creates lasting behavioural abilities. Territories emerge near actual groups of inhabitants. Rest, Gather, and Exchange are equally likely; sustained inhabitation establishes regions. Bureaucracy and Solidarity influence region emergence and establishment without adding inhabitants. Established places protect against outer-wall encroachment without restricting passage. The enclosure starts at roughly 85% of the canvas and gently contracts unless inhabitants push back.

How it works

Interior imprints mirror the inhabitant that made them: Growers leave branching forms, Builders leave coils, and Movers leave waves. Exchange experience makes imprints more likely and substantially amplifies each species’ wall-making action. Imprints fade unless renewed.

Growers extend branching shelters at the boundary, making more room for other inhabitants.

Builders create layered recesses and sharp corners, creating new niches and encouraging more elaborate coils.

Movers wear passages and generate rippled edges. Their waveforms respond to wall complexity and nearby movement.

No inhabitants die or harm one another. When a place becomes crowded, inhabitants adapt, seek different spaces, and collectively encourage the region to expand. Reproduction pauses at the population limit; existing inhabitants remain. Offspring inherit modified traits.

Research connections: Gordon Pask / Usman Haque: reciprocal feedback and conversation; Nicholas Negroponte: responsive environments; Kisho Kurokawa: metabolism and ongoing renewal. The model’s regions, spatial memory, establishment thresholds, and numerical rules are original speculative interpretations, not rules extracted from those texts. It models noncompetitive cohabitation and mutual adaptation, not a validated ecological or physical prediction.

03 / STRONG FIT · CYBERNETICS

The Architectural Relevance of Gordon Pask

Haque’s account of Pask treats environments as participants in ongoing conversations. That is a close match for asking how architecture might learn from non-human users rather than only act on their behalf. The supplied excerpt specifically describes shared environments and participants learning from one another.

Usman Haque, “The Architectural Relevance of Gordon Pask,” Architectural Design 77, no. 4 (2007). Verify page range and issue details against the supplied reading.

04 / STRONG FIT · MODELS & REPRESENTATION

The Inner Life of Models

Wiscombe and Trotter argue that models are shaped by deliberate choices about scale, material, and detail. That makes this reading especially useful for examining how a design model can make some users’ needs legible while leaving others out—the core challenge in representing non-human futures.

Tom Wiscombe and Marrikka Trotter, “The Inner Life of Models” (2020). Supplied course PDF; verify publication venue and pagination against the published text.

The original panel was removed at that stage. No PDF upload or storage feature is included. This records the group’s design choices rather than attributing them to AI.
