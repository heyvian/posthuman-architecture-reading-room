How it works

Resources are shown as pale green dots. Each dot represents a wedge of the environment, shared by inhabitants in that wedge. A larger supply can draw them toward an area, support wellbeing, and help reproduction. Abundance changes how quickly the supply recovers; Exchange regions also replenish it.

Interior imprints sit on a hexagonal grid and mirror the inhabitant that made them: Growers leave branching forms, Builders leave coils, and Movers leave waves. Exchange experience makes imprints more likely and substantially amplifies each species’ wall-making action. Imprints fade unless renewed; weaker traces fade faster.

Growers extend branching shelters at the boundary. Sustained contact with strong branches or interior imprints can add small areas to neighboring region edges.

Builders create layered recesses and sharp corners, creating new niches and encouraging more elaborate coils. Strong layers and coil imprints can gradually erode the parts of regions they touch; wall layers act faster than imprints.

Movers wear passages and generate rippled edges. Their waveforms respond to wall complexity and nearby movement.

No inhabitants die or harm one another. At the outer wall, they have an equal chance of following it or deflecting away. In crowded places they can seek other spaces, while activity encourages regions to grow. Each species can have up to 20 inhabitants; reproduction pauses at that limit. Offspring inherit traits and some individual memories.

Living regions

● Rest

Inhabitants slow down and glow here. Their memories of rest help them travel faster, even after they leave.

● Gather

Inhabitants are drawn toward neighbours. Gather memory encourages them to stay near others of their species.

● Exchange

Inhabitants vibrate here, and shared resources regenerate. Exchange memory strengthens their imprints and influence on the wall.

How regions evolve

Formation. Regions emerge where inhabitants gather, up to 36 in total, including established regions. New regions fit around existing ones; neighboring regions of the same type can merge and count as one.

Change. Use gradually changes a region's size and shape. A frequently occupied region can become established; sustained changes in use can eventually change its type. High Impermanence makes this more responsive to smaller groups. Bureaucracy and Solidarity affect how regions form and become established.

Growth and erosion. Strong Grower traces can add small areas at an edge. Sustained Builder traces can erode an edge—even of an established region—and may remove a small region entirely. Wall contact acts faster than an imprint.

Unless eroded away, regions remain when unoccupied. Regions do not block movement, and the outer wall stays outside their footprints. Each region also changes each species' body differently, as shown in the comparison beside the simulation.

Recent events

Research connections: Adaptability explores an analogy with reciprocal change in Haque’s account of Pask; Impermanence draws on Jencks’s account of Metabolism and renewal; Solidarity explores overlapping relationships in Alexander’s city. These are speculative extensions, not rules extracted from the texts. The simulation’s programmed responses are not Pask’s conversation theory, and its noncompetitive cohabitation and mutual adaptation are not validated ecological predictions.

03 / CYBERNETICS

The Architectural Relevance of Gordon Pask

Haque’s account of Pask treats environments as participants in ongoing conversations. It opens a way to ask how architecture might learn from non-human users rather than only act on their behalf.

Usman Haque, “The Architectural Relevance of Gordon Pask,” Architectural Design 77, no. 4 (2007).

04 / MODELS & REPRESENTATION

The Inner Life of Models

Wiscombe and Trotter describe models as shaped by choices about scale, material, and detail. Their reading raises a question for non-human futures: whose needs become legible in a design model, and whose are left out?

Tom Wiscombe and Marrikka Trotter, “The Inner Life of Models,” Log 50 (Fall 2020): 91–102.

FEATURED COURSE SOURCES

Editorial selection

From the group’s supplied readings, AI highlighted Haque’s essay on Gordon Pask and Wiscombe and Trotter’s “The Inner Life of Models” under “Sources in conversation.” They were selected for their direct relevance: one frames environments as participants in reciprocal cybernetic conversations, while the other makes explicit that models select what they represent. The annotations connect those ideas to the group’s question without presenting the authors as directly writing about non-human architectural users.

DESIGN & EDITORIAL DECISIONS

Human direction

The group described a visual card pile, search and taxonomy filters, slide-out reading details, citations, and a public-facing learning goal. It then specified the interaction: selecting one card should lift it from a slightly blurred pile, show three related readings from different categories for cross-pollination, and keep the detail drawer on the right. The group later asked for the pile to shuffle on each page load so the same cards would not always sit on top. It explicitly chose not to host PDFs, asking instead for information, quotations, and citations. The group rejected the speculative “Working position” copy and asked to remove the original cybernetics configurator panel as confusing.

AI response

The app keeps cards, search, filters, the selected-card view, cross-category recommendations, and the detail drawer; the pile order is randomized once per page load and remains stable while visitors search and filter. The original panel was removed at that stage. No PDF upload or storage feature is included. This records the group’s design choices rather than attributing them to AI.

A HISTORY OF BECOMING · CO-EVOLUTION & READINGS

Human direction

The project owner asked for an environment and inhabitants that visibly change one another over time, rather than a fixed architectural diagram. They directed the development of organic, non-overlapping regions; merging, growth and edge erosion; species-shaped imprints; a smaller starting enclosure with room to grow; and a comparison between initial and current generated inhabitants. They also suggested connections to the Situationists and the Metabolists.

AI development and design choices

AI implemented three kinds of inhabitant—Growers, Builders and Movers—and Rest, Gather and Exchange regions. Inhabitants leave traces and reshape the wall; time in regions changes their forms and behavior. Individual memories fade, while repeated encounters slowly shift a species-wide inherited baseline during a run. Regions can emerge, change shape or type, merge, gain small additions, or erode at their edges. This makes space both a product of and an influence on imagined non-human inhabitants, rather than a static backdrop. Shared resources, adjustable conditions, species comparisons and history make parts of this feedback visible. The owner requested clearer explanations and performance improvements; AI reorganized the guide and reduced redundant drawing while retaining the simulation rules.

Readings in conversation

Usman Haque, “The Architectural Relevance of Gordon Pask.” Its account of environments and participants learning from one another offers a cybernetic lens for the model’s reciprocal feedback. The simulation uses programmed rules; it is not itself a demonstration of Pask’s conversation theory.

Charles Jencks’s introduction to Kisho Kurokawa’s Metabolism in Architecture. Jencks describes Metabolism through a biological analogy. The model’s changing regions and enclosure explore architecture as something that develops over time, without reproducing a Metabolist proposal or an actual biological process.

Constant and Guy Debord, “Amsterdam Declaration.” The group-provided image of selected passages suggests a connection to actively constructing environments rather than treating them as neutral containers. The model extends that question to imagined non-human inhabitants; this is the group’s interpretive direction, not a claim that the declaration itself centers non-human users.

Tom Wiscombe and Marrikka Trotter, “The Inner Life of Models.” Their discussion of models as selective constructions helps make the simulation’s limits visible: its three species, resources, rules and peaceful coexistence are design choices, not a complete account of ecological life.

Interpretation

These connections are interpretations rather than claims that the readings prescribe the model’s specific mechanics. The simulation is a speculative prompt for discussion, not a validated ecological or physical prediction.
