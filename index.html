Theory and context

Introduction

In the late 1950’s and early 1960’s, the architectural movement of Metabolism and the situationists’ Amsterdam Declaration introduced new ideas for architecture and urbanism that rejected god’s eye view planning in favour of personal agency and participatory design. In this post-war climate, designers imagined a world in which people – who have diverse identities and needs – have the power to shape their environments, resulting in systems that live, die, regenerate and reconfigure themselves over time. The thinkers of the time imagined that these systems would in turn shape the people who engaged with them, creating a never-ending feedback loop between the environment and its inhabitants. Kurokawa believed that peoples’ engagement with his modular architectural system would result in a cultural shift to the extent that people would “lose the age-old desire to own possessions and stately homes” and the nuclear family would be replaced by people living more individualistic lifestyles (). While not all of the predictions and aspirations of the metabolists and situationists have been realized, these ideas are still current today, especially as designers seek ways to address and adapt to our changing climate and respond to the integration of AI in the design of the built environment.

The interactive model

App Description and Functionality

This app is a conceptual simulation in which users can establish an initial spatial framework and set environmental and systemic conditions that entities within the system can interact with. The entities are symbolic actors that are representative of real user groups that exist within the built environment. They could be thought of as different demographics of people, more than human users, organisms that could evolve in a future post-human environment, or a combination of all of these.

The systemic and environmental conditions that users can manipulate include bureaucracy, innovation, abundance, solidarity, adaptability and impermanence. The sliders can be used to determine the level of intensity each parameter exerts on the behaviour of the entities and the development of the architecture. These parameters are related to real-world systems that shape how the built environment develops, and how designers respond. For example, the metabolist architect Kurokawa aimed to enable people to live free from the constraints of bureaucracy through his modular and adaptable buildings that were developed during politically and environmentally unstable times in post-war Japan (). Guy Debord and the situationists, who were also responding to post-war conditions of elevated consumerism and top-down urban planning principles, sought to empower people to have more agency in how their surroundings evolve by encouraging participatory urbanism, adaptability and impermanence (). The innovation, abundance and solidarity parameters bring in current challenges facing the built environment including climate change and AI. In her writing, Robin Wall Kimmerer address how humans could be interacting with the environment in a more reciprocal way, which would involve more thoughtful planning of resource use and greater solidary both amongst humans and between humans and greater than human beings.

The app invites users to consider the constraints and conditions that designers have been grappling with and seeking to influence through history, and to observe how different combinations can result in different outcomes for the entities within the system. The app should lead us to question, how much agency individual people have over their environments and how much agency designers can and should have when it comes to designing our environments for ourselves and others.

How it works

Resources are shown as pale green dots. Each dot represents a wedge of the environment, shared by inhabitants in that wedge. A larger supply can draw them toward an area, support wellbeing, and help reproduction. Abundance changes how quickly the supply recovers; Exchange regions also replenish it.

Interior imprints sit on a hexagonal grid and mirror the inhabitant that made them: Growers leave branching forms, Builders leave coils, and Movers leave waves. Exchange experience makes imprints more likely and substantially amplifies each species’ wall-making action. Imprints fade unless renewed; weaker traces fade faster.

Growers extend branching shelters at the boundary. Sustained contact with strong branches or interior imprints can add small areas to neighboring region edges.

Builders create layered recesses and sharp corners, creating new niches and encouraging more elaborate coils. Strong layers and coil imprints can gradually erode the parts of regions they touch; wall layers act faster than imprints.

Movers wear passages and generate rippled edges. Their waveforms respond to wall complexity and nearby movement.

No inhabitants die or harm one another. At the outer wall, they have an equal chance of following it or deflecting away. In crowded places they can seek other spaces, while activity encourages regions to grow. Each species can have up to 20 inhabitants; reproduction pauses at that limit. Offspring inherit traits and some individual memories.

Reading the outcome

Room placement, previous conditions and chance encounters shape what emerges, so no single setting determines the result. Read alongside and , the model invites reflection on how spaces and inhabitants can change together. It illustrates programmed relationships, not evidence of how real species experience architecture.

How regions evolve

Formation. Regions emerge where inhabitants gather, up to 36 in total, including established regions. New regions fit around existing ones; neighboring regions of the same type can merge and count as one.

Change. Use gradually changes a region's size and shape. A frequently occupied region can become established; sustained changes in use can eventually change its type. High Impermanence makes this more responsive to smaller groups. Bureaucracy and Solidarity affect how regions form and become established.

Growth and erosion. Strong Grower traces can add small areas at an edge. Sustained Builder traces can erode an edge—even of an established region—and may remove a small region entirely. Wall contact acts faster than an imprint.

Unless eroded away, regions remain when unoccupied. Regions do not block movement, and the outer wall stays outside their footprints. Each region also changes each species' body differently, as shown in the comparison beside the simulation.

Recent events

Reflection

Authorship and Use of AI

When developing the app for users to interact with, we found that AI was an apt tool for the exploration of these ideas. It is a contemporary tool that has already begun to be used to shape our built environment, and AI is its own form of metabolist system that learns, adapts and responds to changing conditions. The app is an “architectural machine” that, when given inputted forms, learns and develops design criteria, then creates new forms as a result, as described by Negroponte (). We have directed the AI to perform operations that manipulate both the inhabitants of the system and the system itself relative to one another, but we are unaware of all the content that the AI has been trained on, and the decision-making process of the AI is fundamentally unknown to us. We can predetermine the behaviour of this simulation to an extent based on the context parameters that we have established, but the outcome of the system is always unexpected. In this way, the use of AI has added a layer of entropy to the process that is analogous to the unruliness of the systems described by the metabolists and situationists. Though the use of AI is meant to strengthen our concept in this symbolic way, we also hope that users of the app will reflect on their own level of agency in design, especially within the context of emerging AI use in the field. Not only does our app confront users with questions of agency, it also encourages users to critically investigate factors that fundamentally influence own their design thinking.

DESIGN & EDITORIAL DECISIONS

Human direction

The project owner described a visual card pile, search and taxonomy filters, slide-out reading details, citations, and a public-facing learning goal. The owner then specified the interaction: selecting one card should lift it from a slightly blurred pile, show three related readings from different categories for cross-pollination, and keep the detail drawer on the right. The owner later asked for the pile to shuffle on each page load so the same cards would not always sit on top. They explicitly chose not to host PDFs, asking instead for information, quotations, and citations. AI response

AI implemented the cards, search, filters, selected-card view, cross-category recommendations, and detail drawer. The pile order is randomized once per page load and remains stable while visitors search and filter. The original panel was removed at that stage. No PDF upload or storage feature is included.

A HISTORY OF BECOMING · CO-EVOLUTION & READINGS

Human direction

The project owner asked for an environment and inhabitants that visibly change one another over time, rather than a fixed architectural diagram. They directed the development of organic, non-overlapping regions; merging, growth and edge erosion; species-shaped imprints; a smaller starting enclosure with room to grow; and a comparison between initial and current generated inhabitants. They also suggested connections to the Situationists and the Metabolists.

AI development and design choices

AI reimplemented and extended the supplied prototype, including its Growers, Builders and Movers and Rest, Gather and Exchange regions. Inhabitants leave traces and reshape the wall; time in regions changes their forms and behavior. Individual memories fade, while repeated encounters slowly shift a species-wide inherited baseline during a run. Regions can emerge, change shape or type, merge, gain small additions, or erode at their edges. This makes space both a product of and an influence on imagined non-human inhabitants, rather than a static backdrop. Shared resources, adjustable conditions, species comparisons and history make parts of this feedback visible. The owner requested clearer explanations and performance improvements; AI reorganized the guide and reduced redundant drawing while retaining the simulation rules.

Readings in conversation

Usman Haque, “The Architectural Relevance of Gordon Pask.” Its account of environments and participants learning from one another offers a cybernetic lens for the model’s reciprocal feedback. The simulation uses programmed rules; it is not itself a demonstration of Pask’s conversation theory.

Charles Jencks’s introduction to Kisho Kurokawa’s Metabolism in Architecture. Jencks describes Metabolism through a biological analogy. The model’s changing regions and enclosure explore architecture as something that develops over time, without reproducing a Metabolist proposal or an actual biological process.

Constant and Guy Debord, “Amsterdam Declaration.” The owner-provided image of selected passages suggests a connection to actively constructing environments rather than treating them as neutral containers. The model extends that question to imagined non-human inhabitants; this is a project interpretation, not a claim that the declaration itself centers non-human users.

Tom Wiscombe and Marrikka Trotter, “The Inner Life of Models.” Their discussion of models as selective constructions helps make the simulation’s limits visible: its three species, resources, rules and peaceful coexistence are design choices, not a complete account of ecological life.

Interpretation

These connections are interpretations rather than claims that the readings prescribe the model’s specific mechanics. The simulation is a speculative prompt for discussion, not a validated ecological or physical prediction.

Prompts, revisions, and corrections

  1. Cross-pollination: The project owner asked for cards to feel “more visually a pile of cards on the table. Not a grid” and for three related cards from different categories: “Forces cross pollination.” AI revised the card layout and selection view to bring different fields into conversation.
  2. Rejecting an unclear model: The owner said, “I really don't think this section ‘Give the building a signal. See what it does.’ makes sense at all” and asked for the feedback-loop panel to be removed. AI removed it; the current simulation was subsequently developed from a separate owner-supplied prototype.
  3. Spaces that change: The owner asked that regions not overlap and that “the new one forms around the existing one.” AI revised region formation and added ways for regions to change with use. The owner later chose a limit of 36 regions; the model also allows neighboring regions of the same type to merge.
  4. Inhabitants changed by space: The owner asked, “Maybe over time the environement and regions permanently alter the base generation.” AI added a species-wide inherited baseline influenced by exposure and a comparison of initial and current generated forms. These are speculative rules for illustrating reciprocal change, not claims about biological evolution.
  5. Setting initial conditions: The owner asked to “Allow the users to place the first of each room type before the simulation begins.” AI added placement of Exchange, Gather, and Rest rooms before playback, making the visitor’s influence on the starting arrangement visible while leaving later changes to the model’s interactions.
  6. Connecting controls and readings: The owner asked, “I think the slider info should just go right below the slider. Maybe it's an accordion reveal of ‘topic connection’ or something like that but I like what you've done”. AI moved reading links and explanations under each condition in expandable panels, replacing the separate connections grid.

These examples document the project conversation, not work completed outside it.
